01 Research

Which genes let some people live to a hundred?

People who reach very old ages tend to postpone or escape many age-related diseases, which makes them one of the most informative natural experiments in human biology. Genetic studies keep finding signals near a few genes such as APOE and FOXO3, but most of the heritable part of survival is still unexplained.

Three things make the search hard. First, the signal is weak and the cohorts are small: lifespan is only modestly heritable, and studies of long-lived people have thousands of participants, not the hundreds of thousands behind studies of common diseases. Second, long-lived people and their controls are hard to match. They are often recruited separately, born decades apart and sometimes drawn from different populations, so a variant can look linked to longevity only because it marks ancestry or how a group was recruited. Standard statistical corrections handle this well in large, well-matched studies, and less well in small ones. Third, most associated variants lie outside genes, in regulatory DNA, so even a real signal rarely says which gene it acts on, or how.

Our approach at LCAD is to describe each person's genome by what it is predicted to do, using Google DeepMind's sequence-to-function model AlphaGenome, which reads DNA, regulatory regions included, and predicts gene activity. We then rank genes by how much of a trait that predicted activity explains. The model brings in what it learned from large functional-genomics datasets, which a small cohort cannot provide, and each score is tied to a specific gene by construction. Before pointing this at longevity, we test it on traits where the right answer is known.

Roadmap

  1. Step 1 · Representation

    Do the predicted signals carry real biology?

    From AlphaGenome's predicted expression over 11 pigmentation genes, a CNN tells apart the five continental superpopulations of 1000 Genomes at 84% accuracy (XGBoost 81%). One known pigmentation gene, OCA2, alone reaches 64%; a random gene reaches 37%.

    ICANN 2026 · oral, presented by me

  2. Step 2 · Discovery under confounding

    Can it find the right genes when ancestry and trait coincide?

    A deliberately extreme test: skin pigmentation in a cohort where it matches ancestry exactly, so no correction can separate the two and association tests cannot tell causal genes from ancestry markers. Ranking 9 known pigmentation genes against 33 controls, our method reaches AUC 0.880 versus 0.724 for a variant-level association test. Among the top 5 genes, ours ranks 4 known pigmentation genes; the association test ranks 2.

    Manuscript under review · first author

  3. Step 3 · Mechanism

    Which variants inside a top-ranked gene matter?

    Using AlphaGenome to mutate sequences in silico and see which changes move the predicted expression, turning a ranked gene into a testable hypothesis.

    In progress

  4. Step 4 · Longevity

    Apply the validated pipeline to long-lived people

    Run the same pipeline on cohorts labeled by longevity, such as centenarians against controls, while controlling for ancestry, and check its candidates against known longevity genes.

    Next

Questions I want to work on

  • Small cohorts. How far can priors learned by sequence-to-function models stand in for the hundreds of thousands of genomes that disease studies use?
  • Confounding. Our pigmentation testbed is the extreme case, where trait and ancestry are identical. How much of the method's advantage survives in real longevity cohorts, where the mismatch between cases and controls is milder but still there?
  • Tissues and time. In which cell types, and at what point in life, does a longevity gene act? I'm getting practice with gene expression over time through the Virtual Embryo Challenge, with my lab's team.
